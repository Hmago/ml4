## Simple Explanation

![Infographic: your brain is part of your body. Shows the seven levers ranked biggest first (Move, Eat, Sleep, Train, Calm, Connect, Supplement), a brain fed by blood, fuel and sleep, a cognitive-reserve gauge (Stern 2012), key numbers (about 45% of dementia potentially preventable or delayable across 14 modifiable risk factors, Livingston 2024; 1-5 IQ points per extra year of school), neuroplasticity studies (taxi drivers, trainees over about 4 years, jugglers in 3 months) and what you can and cannot change.](diagrams/brain05b_info_brain_body_reserve_ai.png)

Your brain is not a fixed machine you're stuck with — it's a **living organ that keeps rewiring itself based on how you use and look after it**. This is called *neuroplasticity*, and it never fully switches off, even in your 70s.

Here's the key idea most "brain hack" articles get wrong: **the brain is part of your body.** It runs on the blood your heart pumps, the fuel your food provides, and the repair work your sleep performs. So the biggest levers for a sharper mind are not exotic pills — they're the same things that keep the rest of you healthy, just aimed at your head:

```
   WHAT KEEPS A BRAIN SHARP AND AGING SLOWLY  (biggest levers first)

     1. MOVE        exercise boosts blood flow and growth factors;
                    it protects the memory centre
     2. EAT         a heart-healthy pattern protects the blood vessels
                    the brain runs on
     3. SLEEP       the brain files memories (and probably clears waste)
     4. TRAIN       effortful learning rewires you for the skills you practise
     5. CALM        chronic stress harms the memory centre; calming protects it
     6. CONNECT     people, novelty, and purpose build "spare capacity"
     7. SUPPLEMENT  a small final polish — mostly for fixing a real deficiency
```

Notice supplements are **last and smallest**. That order is the whole message of this chapter: the "boring" habits (moving, eating, sleeping) beat every powder in the store, and they're free. Pills are the garnish, not the meal.

## Official Definition

> **Neuroplasticity** is the brain's lifelong ability to reorganize its structure, function, and connections in response to experience. Practice physically changes the brain: London taxi drivers have a larger rear part of the memory centre (posterior hippocampus), and the longer they have driven, the larger it is (Maguire et al., 2000, *PNAS*); trainees who spent about four years learning the city's streets and *qualified* grew that region, while those who failed did not (Woollett & Maguire, 2011, *Current Biology*). Adults who learned to juggle gained grey matter in motion-processing areas within three months — and it shrank back after they stopped (Draganski et al., 2004, *Nature*). The brain you use is the brain you keep.

> **Cognitive reserve** is your brain's buffer against aging and damage — built up by education, mentally demanding work, exercise, and a rich social and intellectual life. People with more reserve can sustain more physical brain aging before it shows up as decline (Stern, 2012, *Lancet Neurology*). That is what "younger" means in this chapter: you can't stop the clock, but you can build a bigger buffer and remove the things that speed the clock up.

> ⚠️ **Honest expectation-setting — read this before anything else.** No food, app, or supplement permanently raises your IQ by 15 points. Your IQ *relative to people your age* is very stable through adulthood; the best-proven lever is schooling (about 1–5 IQ points per extra year of education — Ritchie & Tucker-Drob, 2018, *Psychological Science*), and "brain-training" games do **not** transfer to general intelligence (Owen et al., 2010, *Nature*; Melby-Lervåg & Hulme, 2013; Simons et al., 2016). What you genuinely **can** do is: (1) get your existing brain working at its true ceiling (most people run it under-slept, under-moved, and poorly fed), (2) sharpen the *specific* skills you practise, and (3) **lower your risk** of later decline — the Lancet Commission estimates that about **45% of dementia cases worldwide are potentially preventable or delayable** by tackling 14 modifiable risk factors (Livingston et al., 2024, *Lancet*; a population estimate, not a personal guarantee). Those gains are real, modest, and they compound. Anyone promising a quick IQ jump is selling snake oil.

> **In plain English:** think of it like fitness. You can't turn a 5-foot-6 person into a 6-foot-2 NBA player — but almost *anyone* can go from unfit and sluggish to strong and energetic with training. Same with your brain: not a new engine, but a well-tuned, well-fuelled, well-rested one that stays in good shape for longer than a neglected one. That difference is worth a lot, and it's available to you.

### How Big Are the Effects? What the Large Trials Show

Single studies make headlines; **large randomized trials** tell you the real size of the effect. These four anchor everything in this chapter:

| Study | Who and how long | What happened | What it means for you |
|-------|------------------|---------------|-----------------------|
| **FINGER** (Ngandu et al., *Lancet*, 2015) | 1,260 at-risk adults aged 60–77, 2 years | Diet + exercise + brain training + blood-pressure/cholesterol checks beat general health advice on a cognitive test battery (difference 0.022 SD per year) | The first big proof that a *combined* lifestyle program helps — modestly |
| **US POINTER** (Baker et al., *JAMA*, 2025) | 2,111 at-risk, inactive adults aged 60–79, 2 years | Both groups improved; the structured, coached program beat the self-guided one: 0.243 vs 0.213 SD per year (difference 0.029) | Structure and accountability add a small extra benefit |
| **MIND diet trial** (Barnes et al., *NEJM*, 2023) | 604 adults aged 65+ with a family history of dementia, 3 years | MIND diet vs a control diet (both with a mild calorie cut): **no significant difference** in cognition or brain MRI | One pillar alone, over 3 years, may show nothing measurable |
| **Lancet Commission** (Livingston et al., 2024) | Population model, 14 risk factors | About 45% of dementia potentially preventable or delayable; new in 2024: high LDL cholesterol and untreated vision loss | Most of the payoff runs through heart health, hearing, vision, activity, and social life (full list: "What to CUT" below) |

The US POINTER "recipe" for the structured group was: 30–35 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous aerobic exercise 4 times a week plus strength and stretching twice a week, the MIND diet, 30 minutes of computer brain training 3 times a week plus social and intellectual activities, and regular blood-pressure, weight, and lab check-ins. There was no "do nothing" group, so part of both groups' rise is simply getting better at the tests.

> **Key number:** **0.029 SD per year** — US POINTER's extra benefit of a structured program over going it alone. Real but small: a healthy lifestyle *protects* the brain; it doesn't *transform* it.

## Pillar 1 — MOVE: Exercise Is the Closest Thing to a Smart Pill 🟢

![Infographic: MOVE, exercise as the closest thing to a smart pill (strong evidence). Shows the cause chain move, more blood and oxygen, BDNF released, new hippocampus connections and less shrinkage; the WHO 2020 prescription (150-300 min moderate or 75-150 min vigorous, strength 2+ days, age 65+ balance on 3+ days, moderate means you can talk but not sing); the rule weekly minutes = moderate + 2 x vigorous of at least 150; a worked table (5 x 30-min walks 150, 3 x 25-min jogs 150, 2 jogs + 2 walks 160 meet WHO; 3 x 20-min walks 60 is short by 90 min and 2 strength days); and evidence (Erickson 2011 +2% front hippocampus, Singh 2025 cognition SMD 0.42).](diagrams/brain05b_info_move_exercise_prescription_ai.png)

> **Simple version:** If there were one pill that sharpened thinking a little, lifted mood, protected the memory centre, and lowered dementia risk, it would be the biggest drug in history. That "pill" exists — it's called **exercise**, and it's free. Movement has some of the strongest evidence in this whole chapter.

> **Official:** Aerobic exercise raises **BDNF** (brain-derived neurotrophic factor), a protein that helps neurons survive, grow, and connect. The nickname *"Miracle-Gro for the brain"* was popularised by psychiatrist John Ratey (*Spark*, 2008); the mostly-animal evidence was reviewed by Cotman & Berchtold (2002, *Trends in Neurosciences*), and in humans a single session moderately raises BDNF in the blood (Szuhany et al., 2015, meta-analysis of 29 studies). In the landmark randomized trial, walking 40 minutes 3 times a week for a year **grew the front of the hippocampus by about 2%** in older adults — "effectively reversing age-related loss in volume by 1 to 2 years" — while the stretching-and-toning control group's shrank (Erickson et al., 2011, *PNAS*; 120 adults).

### What the evidence says (in numbers)

| Study | What it found |
|-------|---------------|
| Erickson et al., 2011 (*PNAS*; RCT, 120 older adults, 1 year) | Walking: front hippocampus +2%; control group shrank; bigger growth went with better spatial memory |
| Firth et al., 2018 (*NeuroImage*; 14 trials, 737 people) | No significant change in *total* hippocampus volume; exercise mainly *prevents* shrinkage (left hippocampus) |
| Northey et al., 2018 (*BJSM*; 39 studies, adults over 50) | Thinking improved (effect size 0.29); aerobic, resistance, mixed training, and tai chi all helped; sessions of 45–60 min at least moderate intensity |
| Singh et al., 2025 (*BJSM*; umbrella review, 2,724 RCTs, ~258,000 people) | General cognition +0.42, memory +0.26, executive function +0.24 (effect sizes); even light intensity helped |
| Ding et al., 2025 (*Lancet Public Health*; observational) | 7,000 vs 2,000 steps a day: 38% lower dementia risk (only 2 studies) |

*How to read an effect size (SMD):* about 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large. So exercise gives **small-to-moderate** thinking gains — real, reliable, not magic. The hippocampus "growth" headline is less certain than the cognition result; "protects against shrinkage" is the safer summary.

### Why it works (in plain English)

```
   EXERCISE → SHARPER BRAIN  (the chain of cause and effect)

   You move  →  more blood + oxygen to the brain
             →  release of BDNF (a growth factor)
             →  new connections in the hippocampus (and, in animals,
                new neurons)
             →  better memory, focus, mood; less age-related shrinkage
```

A **single** bout of exercise gives a small, short-lived boost to thinking (Chang et al., 2012, *Brain Research*; 79 studies, average effect about 0.1) — a handy primer before studying or an interview, not a magic switch.

### What to actually do

```
   THE WEEKLY MOVEMENT PRESCRIPTION  (WHO 2020 guidelines for adults)

   AEROBIC (the big one)   150–300 min/week moderate
                             e.g., 5 × 30-min brisk walks = 150
                           OR 75–150 min/week vigorous
                             e.g., 3 × 25-min jogs = 75
                           OR a mix: 1 vigorous min ≈ 2 moderate min
                           → "moderate" = you can talk but not sing

   STRENGTH                2+ days/week, all major muscle groups
                           (bodyweight squats/push-ups count; no gym needed)

   AGE 65+                 add balance + strength ("multicomponent")
                           activity on 3+ days/week to prevent falls

   LESS SITTING            WHO: limit sedentary time (no exact threshold);
                           practical habit — stand or walk every 30–60 min

   STEPS (optional)        ~7,000/day is a realistic target (Ding 2025)
```

> **Rule of thumb:** 150–300 minutes of moderate activity a week (or half that vigorous; 1 vigorous minute ≈ 2 moderate minutes) **plus** strength work on 2 or more days. Some is better than none; more is better than some.

**Check your own week.** The WHO target is a *sum*, so mixing walks and jogs works. This toy checker applies the 1 vigorous = 2 moderate rule to the chapter's examples:

```python
# Checks a week of activity against the WHO 2020 adult targets (1 vigorous min counts as 2 moderate)
TARGET_MIN, TARGET_STRENGTH = 150, 2

def check(name, moderate, vigorous, strength_days):
    equivalent = sum(moderate) + 2 * sum(vigorous)
    short = max(0, TARGET_MIN - equivalent)
    ok = short == 0 and strength_days >= TARGET_STRENGTH
    verdict = "meets WHO" if ok else f"short by {short} min and {max(0, TARGET_STRENGTH - strength_days)} strength days"
    print(f"{name:<26} {equivalent:>3} min-equivalent, {strength_days} strength days -> {verdict}")
    return ok

walks = check("5 x 30-min brisk walks", [30] * 5, [], 2)
jogs = check("3 x 25-min jogs", [], [25] * 3, 2)
mixed = check("2 jogs + 2 walks", [30, 30], [25, 25], 2)
busy = check("3 x 20-min walks", [20] * 3, [], 0)
assert walks and jogs and mixed and not busy
```

**Output:**

```
5 x 30-min brisk walks     150 min-equivalent, 2 strength days -> meets WHO
3 x 25-min jogs            150 min-equivalent, 2 strength days -> meets WHO
2 jogs + 2 walks           160 min-equivalent, 2 strength days -> meets WHO
3 x 20-min walks            60 min-equivalent, 0 strength days -> short by 90 min and 2 strength days
```

**Real examples of "moderate aerobic" that count:** a fast walk while on a phone call, cycling to the shops, a dance class, swimming, an energetic game with your kids, mowing the lawn briskly. It does **not** have to be a gym or a marathon — consistency beats intensity.

> **If you do only ONE thing from this whole chapter:** take a brisk 30-minute walk most days. It's one of the highest-return, lowest-cost brain upgrades there is.

## Pillar 2 — EAT: Feed the Brain Its Raw Materials 🟢 / 🟡

![Infographic: EAT, feed the brain its raw materials (strong evidence for heart and vessels, mixed for brain foods). Compares observational links (MIND diet top vs bottom third like 7.5 years younger, Morris 2015; about 1 serving of leafy greens a day like 11 years younger, Morris 2018; more ultra-processed food 28% faster decline) with the NEJM MIND diet trial (Barnes 2023, 604 adults aged 65+, 3 years, no significant difference, 0.035 units, P = 0.23). Shows the brain-healthy plate (1/2 vegetables and fruit, 1/4 protein, 1/4 whole grains, a little olive oil, water 6-8 glasses a day), MIND targets (greens 6+, berries 2+, fish 1+, nuts 5+ per week, beans 4+ meals a week) and what to dial down (sugar, alcohol at 14-21 UK units a week with 3x hippocampus-shrinkage odds, ultra-processed food, trans fats).](diagrams/brain05b_info_eat_brain_healthy_pattern_ai.png)

> **Simple version:** Your brain is about 2% of your body weight but uses about 20% of your body's energy (Raichle & Gusnard, 2002, *PNAS*). It is built largely from fats and protein and runs on a steady supply of blood glucose. You don't need a perfect or expensive diet — you need a **pattern** rich in plants, fish, nuts, and olive oil and low in ultra-processed food. Most of the proven benefit runs through your **blood vessels**: healthy blood pressure, blood sugar, and cholesterol keep the brain's supply lines open.

> **Official:** Large observational studies link Mediterranean-style eating to slower cognitive decline. The **MIND diet** (a blend of the Mediterranean and DASH diets, tuned to "brain-linked" foods) tracked with slower decline — top vs bottom third of adherence was equivalent to being **7.5 years younger** (Morris et al., 2015, *Alzheimer's & Dementia*; 960 older adults followed ~4.7 years). The authors said a trial was needed — and **the trial was null**: over 3 years, 604 older adults on the MIND diet did **no better** in cognition or brain MRI than a control diet with the same mild calorie cut; both groups improved (Barnes et al., 2023, *NEJM*). Other links are also observational: ~1 serving of leafy greens a day (top fifth, median 1.3 servings) went with decline equal to being ~11 years younger (Morris et al., 2018, *Neurology*); more berries went with up to 2.5 years' delay in cognitive aging (Devore et al., 2012, *Annals of Neurology*); and eating more **ultra-processed food** went with a 28% faster decline in global cognition over ~8 years (Gomes Gonçalves et al., 2023, *JAMA Neurology*; 10,775 adults).

**Honest reading:** observational links can be confounded — people who eat greens also tend to exercise more, smoke less, and earn more. So the evidence tag is 🟡 for "brain foods" specifically, but 🟢 for heart and blood-vessel health — and high blood pressure, diabetes, obesity, and high LDL cholesterol are all on the Lancet 2024 dementia risk list.

> **Key number:** a difference of **0.035 standardized units (P = 0.23)** — MIND diet vs control diet after 3 years in the NEJM trial, i.e., no significant effect. Eat this way for your heart and blood vessels; don't expect a brain-only boost.

### The brain-food shortlist (eat more of these)

| Food group | Why it helps | Easy target (MIND diet unless noted) |
|------------|--------------|--------------------------------------|
| 🥬 Leafy greens | Folate, vitamin K, lutein; linked to slower decline | 6+ servings/week (about 1 a day) |
| 🫐 Berries | Flavonoids; linked to slower cognitive aging | 2+ servings/week |
| 🐟 Fatty fish | DHA omega-3 is a structural brain fat | 1+ meal/week (heart guidance: 2/week) |
| 🥜 Nuts | Healthy fats, vitamin E | 5+ servings/week (a small handful) |
| 🫒 Olive oil | Monounsaturated fat | Your main cooking/dressing fat |
| 🫘 Beans & whole grains | Fibre; slow, steady glucose | Beans 4+ meals/week; whole grains 3 servings/day |
| 🥚 Eggs | Choline, a building block of the memory chemical acetylcholine | Up to about 1 a day is fine for most healthy people (AHA advisory, 2020) |
| ☕ Coffee / tea | Caffeine aids alertness (in moderation) | 1–3 cups, not late in the day (daily caffeine limit: Pillar 7) |

### What to cut (these age the brain)

```
   DIAL DOWN  (not "never," just "usually not")

   • Ultra-processed foods  (packaged snacks, sugary cereals, sodas, fast food)
       → linked to faster cognitive decline (observational)
   • Added sugar + refined carbs → there is no real "sugar rush"
       (see the blood-sugar rule below)
   • Alcohol → even moderate drinking (14–21 UK units/week) went with 3× the
       odds of right-hippocampus shrinkage; light drinking was not protective
       (Topiwala et al., 2017, BMJ). Less is better — see "What to CUT".
   • Trans fats and heavily fried food
```

### The plate + the blood-sugar rule

```
   A BRAIN-HEALTHY PLATE (adapted from Harvard's Healthy Eating Plate)

   ┌───────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │  1/2  vegetables (esp. leafy greens) + fruit  │
   │  1/4  protein (fish, beans, eggs, poultry)    │
   │  1/4  whole grains / starchy veg              │
   │   +   a little olive oil, nuts, or seeds      │
   │  drink: water; berries for dessert            │
   └───────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

> **Why blood sugar matters (plain English):** a doughnut doesn't give you a "sugar rush" — a meta-analysis of 31 studies (1,259 people) found sugar didn't lift mood at all, and people felt *more tired and less alert* within the first hour (Mantantzis et al., 2019, *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews*). Meals with fibre and protein release fuel slowly and steadily. And over decades, keeping blood sugar healthy matters more: diabetes is one of the Lancet dementia risk factors. **Steady fuel = steady focus.**

### Don't forget water

Mild dehydration hurts mood and some attention tasks. In small lab studies, losing about 1.4–1.6% of body weight in water increased fatigue and tension and slowed working memory in men (Ganio et al., 2011; 26 men) and worsened mood, concentration, and headaches in women — though most cognitive tests were unaffected (Armstrong et al., 2012; 25 women). A meta-analysis of 33 studies found clear impairment of attention, executive function, and coordination mainly when the loss **exceeds 2%** (Wittbrodt & Millard-Stafford, 2018).

```
   HYDRATION (NHS guidance)
   • Aim for 6–8 cups or glasses of fluid a day — water, milk, tea, and
     coffee all count. More in heat, exercise, or illness.
   • Check: your pee should be a clear pale yellow.
```

## Pillar 3 — SLEEP: When the Brain Repairs and Cleans Itself 🟢

![Infographic on sleep and memory: deep sleep files facts, REM links ideas, waste clearance probable but debated in humans; a one-night timeline from 23:00 to 07:00 with deep blocks early and REM later; rule of thumb 7+ hours, same wake time, last coffee about 9 hours before bed, so bed at 23:00 means last coffee by 14:00; key numbers: 6 h or less at 50, 60 and 70 gives about 30% higher dementia risk (Sabia 2021), about 7 hours best (Li 2022), coffee 8.8 h or more before bed and about 45 min less sleep (Gardiner 2023); protocol checklist, see a doctor for unrefreshing sleep or snoring, CBT-I first for insomnia; trap: alcohol fragments sleep and suppresses REM.](diagrams/brain05b_info_sleep_protocol_ai.png)

> **Simple version:** You don't build muscle in the gym — you build it while resting. Your brain is similar: the studying you do all day *sticks* much better once you've slept on it, and a tired brain is worse at taking in new information the next day. Skimping on sleep doesn't buy you more learning time; it weakens the learning you already did. Sleep is not the opposite of productivity — it's part of it.

> **Official:** During sleep, the brain **consolidates memory** — in deep (slow-wave) sleep it replays the day's learning and helps move it into long-term storage, and REM sleep seems to help link and integrate it (Diekelmann & Born, 2010, *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*; Rasch & Born, 2013, *Physiological Reviews*). Missing sleep hurts both ends: one night without sleep impaired the ability to *form* new memories the next day (Yoo et al., 2007, *Nature Neuroscience* — a small lab study). Sleep may also help **clear waste**: in mice, sleep widened the space between brain cells by 60% and sped clearance of amyloid-β, the protein tied to Alzheimer's (Xie et al., 2013, *Science*). In humans, one night of total sleep loss raised amyloid-β in the hippocampus (Shokri-Kojori et al., 2018, *PNAS*; 20 people) and slowed clearance of an MRI tracer (Eide et al., 2021, *Brain*). How much "washing" happens during *normal* sleep is still debated — a 2024 mouse study even found clearance was *lower* during sleep (Miao et al., *Nature Neuroscience*).

### Why it works

```
   WHAT SLEEP DOES FOR YOUR BRAIN EACH NIGHT

   DEEP SLEEP  →  helps file today's facts into long-term memory
   REM SLEEP   →  helps link ideas; supports emotional balance
   BOTH        →  probably help clear waste proteins
                  (clear in mice; human evidence is small and debated)

   Skip it, and: today's learning is consolidated less well, tomorrow's
   learning is encoded less well, and focus, mood, and willpower drop.
```

**Long-term, in numbers (observational):** people who slept 6 hours or less at ages 50, 60, *and* 70 had about a **30% higher dementia risk** than steady 7-hour sleepers (Sabia et al., 2021, *Nature Communications*; 7,959 people, 25 years). In about half a million UK Biobank adults, roughly **7 hours** was the sweet spot — both shorter and longer sleep went with worse cognition (Li et al., 2022, *Nature Aging*). And a *regular* sleep schedule predicted lower mortality even better than sleep length did (Windred et al., 2024, *Sleep*).

### The sleep protocol (highest-leverage habit here)

```
   NON-NEGOTIABLE SLEEP RULES

   • At least 7 hours (AASM/SRS adult guidance); 7–9 is the usual adult range.
   • FIXED WAKE TIME, 7 days a week — regular timing matters as much as
     duration. A steady wake time anchors your body clock.
   • Bright daylight in the morning (outdoors if you can) sets a strong clock.
   • Last coffee ≥ ~9 hours before bed: a meta-analysis found a regular
     coffee (~107 mg) should be ≥ 8.8 h before bedtime and a pre-workout
     drink (~218 mg) ≥ 13.2 h (Gardiner et al., 2023). On average, caffeine
     cut sleep by about 45 minutes.
   • Dim screens and bright light 30–60 min before bed — reading on a glowing
     e-reader before bed delayed the body clock and cut melatonin compared
     with a printed book (Chang et al., 2015, PNAS).
   • Cool, dark, quiet room. Alcohol helps you fall asleep but fragments the
     second half of the night and suppresses REM.
   • Study the hardest material EARLIER in the day when you're alert; review
     briefly in the evening. That night's sleep consolidates both.
```

> **Rule of thumb:** **7+ hours, the same wake time every day, and the last coffee about 9 hours before bed.**

### Naps and micro-rest

```
   • A 10-min nap gave the best boost after a short night — benefits lasted
     up to ~2.5 hours; a 30-min nap caused grogginess first (Brooks & Lack, 2006).
     Keep naps under ~20–30 min.
   • "Sleep on it": recall of a tough concept is often better the next morning.
   • 10 minutes of quiet wakeful rest right after learning (no phone, no
     podcast) improved memory a week later (Dewar et al., 2012).
```

> ⚠️ If you regularly get 7–9 hours and still wake unrefreshed, snore heavily, or feel exhausted daily, that can signal a treatable **sleep disorder** (e.g., sleep apnea) — see a doctor. If you have **chronic insomnia**, the recommended first treatment is **CBT-I** (cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia), not sleeping pills (American College of Physicians guideline, 2016).

## Pillar 4 — TRAIN: Deliberate Practice That Actually Transfers 🟢

![Infographic map of Pillar 4, deliberate practice: a brain game such as Sudoku makes you better at the same game but not smarter in general (Owen 2010; Simons 2016); a toolbox of six tools - spaced repetition with Anki and FSRS, thinking routines, memory palace, Major system 3-1-4 = M-T-R = MoToR, active reading, a new hard skill; a pick-by-question table for first principles, inversion, Fermi, steelman and calibration; the inversion worked example for a 4-week interview plan turning 3 failure modes into 3 to-dos; evidence from Roediger and Karpicke 2006, Dunlosky 2013 and Park 2014; trap: bulk-importing 500 cards.](diagrams/brain05b_info_train_near_transfer_ai.png)

> **Simple version:** "Use it or lose it" is true — challenging your brain builds and keeps connections. But there's a catch the puzzle-app industry hides: **you only get better at what you actually practise.** Sudoku makes you better at Sudoku, not smarter in general. So this pillar drills the skills you *actually* want — remembering things, reasoning clearly, focusing deeply, learning hard material — because practice on *those* is what pays off.

> **Official:** Practice physically rewires the brain (Draganski et al., 2004; Maguire et al., 2000), but improvements are largely **near-transfer** — they carry to the trained skill and closely related tasks, not to "general IQ" (Owen et al., 2010; Simons et al., 2016). The techniques below train exactly the abilities you want to keep: durable memory (**testing effect + spacing**, Roediger & Karpicke, 2006), clear reasoning, and sustained focus. Learning **genuinely new, demanding skills** (not easy games) improved memory in older adults: in the Synapse Project, learning digital photography and/or quilting for about 16.5 hours a week for 3 months improved episodic memory, while social-only or low-demand activities did not (Park et al., 2014, *Psychological Science*).

> **Rule of thumb:** you get better at what you practise — so practise the real skill (recall, reasoning, the interview itself), not a game that resembles it.

### 4a. Spaced repetition — the single best memory habit

Test yourself on a fact just before you'd forget it, at growing intervals. Use **Anki** (free on desktop, web, and Android; the iPhone app is paid) and turn on its **FSRS** scheduler in deck options — it learns how fast *you* forget. The science, schedules, and FSRS details live in **[Ch 5 §5.2.2 and §5.11](#content/05_brain_training)**; here are the three rules that matter most:

```
   CARD-WRITING RULES
   1. One fact per card, asked as a QUESTION you must recall.
        Good: "Why does exercise protect the hippocampus?"   (something to recall)
        Bad:  "Exercise protects the hippocampus."           (nothing to recall)
   2. If you can't answer in ~10 seconds, the card's too big — split it.
   3. Add just 2–4 of YOUR OWN cards a day. Never bulk-import 500 —
      a deck you dread is a deck you quit.
```

> **Why it beats re-reading:** *trying to recall* a fact (even when you fail) makes it stick far better than reading it again. Highlighting and re-reading were rated **low utility** in the biggest review of study techniques (Dunlosky et al., 2013) — they feel productive but build little. Close the book and test yourself instead.

### 4b. Five reusable thinking routines

Sharp thinking is less about raw horsepower and more about having **routines you run instead of staring blankly.** Keep this card visible:

```
   THINKING-ROUTINE CHEAT CARD

   Stuck on a problem?   → First-Principles: "What do I actually know is true?"
   Making a plan?        → Inversion: "What would GUARANTEE failure?" then avoid it
   Unknown number?       → Fermi: break it into guessable pieces, multiply
   Disagree with X?      → Steelman: state X's STRONGEST version before rejecting
   Predicting outcome?   → Attach a probability; check it later (calibration)
```

**Worked example — Inversion.** Your plan is "prepare for my interview in 4 weeks." Invert it: *"What would guarantee I fail?"* → I only read theory and never practise out loud; I burn out by week 2; I avoid my weak topics. Each failure mode just handed you a to-do: do mock interviews early, schedule rest, start with your weakest area. (More routines and examples in **[Chapter 5](#content/05_brain_training)** and **[Chapter 4 — Aptitude & Mental Math](#content/04_aptitude_mental_math)**.)

### 4c. Memory techniques for when you need them

| Technique | Use it for | The trick |
|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| **Memory palace** (method of loci) | Ordered lists, steps, a talk | Place vivid, absurd images along a route you know (your home) |
| **Major system** | Numbers, dates, constants | Turn digits into sounds → a word → a picture (3-1-4 = M-T-R → "MoToR") |
| **Elaboration + dual coding** | Understanding new ideas | Ask "what does this connect to?" and "what's the picture?" |

These are trainable: after 6 weeks of memory-palace training, ordinary adults' brain networks shifted toward the pattern seen in memory champions, and their recall improved (Dresler et al., 2017, *Neuron*). More mnemonics in **[Ch 5 §5.4](#content/05_brain_training)**.

**Memory-palace example** — the ML pipeline along your hallway: front door → a giant *dataset* pours out (collection); coat rack → coats *scrubbed* clean (preprocessing); kitchen → a chef *splits* a cake into 3 slices (train/val/test); stairs → a robot doing *push-ups* on each step (training); bathroom → a *scale* weighs the robot (evaluation). Walk the route, and the images are waiting.

### 4d. Read hard things, actively

You become what you read: shallow feeds train a twitchy mind; one dense source a week, read *actively*, trains depth.

```
   ACTIVE-READING PROTOCOL (any article, chapter, or paper)
   BEFORE:  Skim headings. Write the 1 question you want answered.   (2 min)
   DURING:  Read in one focused block. Mark only what surprises you.
   AFTER:   Close the source. Write, FROM MEMORY, the 3 key ideas +
            1 thing you didn't understand.  (this is the rep that sticks)
   ENCODE:  Turn those 3 ideas into Anki cards.
```

### 4e. Keep it novel — the anti-"brain-game" rule

The brain is pushed hardest when a task is **new and just beyond comfortable**. So the best "brain training" is **learning a real, demanding skill you don't yet have**: a language, a musical instrument, coding, drawing, dancing. In the Synapse Project it was exactly this kind of demanding learning — not socialising alone — that improved memory. These beat any app because they combine novelty, challenge, and often movement and social contact.

## Pillar 5 — CALM: Protect the Brain From Chronic Stress 🟢 / 🟡

![Infographic on Pillar 5, calm: a short deadline spike of cortisol is fine, but a weeks-long plateau of chronic stress wears on the hippocampus (memory) and prefrontal cortex (focus); key numbers: mindfulness courses cut distress by SMD -0.32 across 13 trials and 2,371 adults (Galante 2023) and breathwork cut stress by g = -0.35 across 12 trials (Fincham 2023); a 2 by 2 toolkit of meditation 10 min a day, cyclic sighing breathing, nature walks and focus boundaries; trial table for Goyal 2014, Whitfield 2022, Balban 2023 and Stevenson 2018; trap: a distracted session is not a failure, every return is a rep.](diagrams/brain05b_info_calm_stress_ai.png)

> **Simple version:** Short bursts of stress are fine — even helpful (a deadline sharpens you). But *chronic*, grinding stress keeps stress hormones such as cortisol high for weeks, and that wears on the exact brain regions you're trying to sharpen: the memory centre and the focus centre. Managing stress isn't self-indulgence; it's maintenance.

> **Official:** Long-term exposure to stress hormones (glucocorticoids such as cortisol) affects the brain structures behind memory and self-control — the **hippocampus** and the **prefrontal cortex** — and the effects depend on when in life the stress happens and how long it lasts (Lupien, McEwen et al., 2009, *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*). Meditation helps, modestly: across 47 randomized trials with active control groups (3,515 people), mindfulness programs had **moderate** evidence for easing anxiety, depression, and pain, only **low** evidence for "stress" itself, and were no better than other active treatments such as exercise or therapy (Goyal et al., 2014, *JAMA Internal Medicine*). Effects on thinking are real but small: across 45 trials, working memory improved by g ≈ 0.23 (Whitfield et al., 2022, *Neuropsychology Review*), and one small trial found a **two-week** course improved working memory and GRE reading scores while reducing mind-wandering (Mrazek et al., 2013, *Psychological Science*).

> **Key number:** group mindfulness courses cut psychological distress by about a third of a standard deviation (SMD −0.32 across 13 trials and 2,371 adults; Galante et al., 2023, *Nature Mental Health*) — a small-to-moderate effect, about the same size as breathwork's effect on stress (g = −0.35 across 12 trials; Fincham et al., 2023, *Scientific Reports*).

### What to do

```
   CALM TOOLKIT (pick 1–2 and be consistent — consistency beats intensity)

   • MEDITATION      10 min/day of focused-attention or breath meditation.
                     Apps or silence both work. The skill you're training is
                     "notice your mind wandered → gently return" — that IS focus.
   • BREATHING       "Cyclic sighing": inhale through the nose, top up with a
                     second short inhale, then a long, slow exhale through the
                     mouth. In a 1-month trial, 5 min/day of it lifted mood more
                     than 5 min/day of mindfulness (Balban et al., 2023).
                     A few cycles also help in an acutely stressful moment.
   • NATURE          A walk in a park or other green space — it can be your
                     Pillar 1 walk. Leave the podcast off.
   • BOUNDARIES      Protect deep-focus blocks; single-task; cut doomscrolling.
```

**Why nature helps:** in a small experiment, a walk in an arboretum improved a working-memory test (repeating digits backwards) more than a walk downtown (Berman et al., 2008, *Psychological Science*), and a 2018 review of 42 studies found small-to-moderate gains in working memory and mental flexibility after time in natural settings (Stevenson et al., 2018). The focus habits behind "Boundaries" (deep work, single-tasking) are taught in **[Chapter 5 — Brain Training](#content/05_brain_training)**.

> **Plain-English meditation reframe:** you're not trying to "empty your mind" (impossible). Every time you notice you drifted and come back, that's **one rep of the attention muscle** — like a bicep curl for focus. A "distracted" session full of returns is a *successful* workout.

> **Watch-out:** if stress has turned into weeks of anxiety, low mood, or broken sleep, a 10-minute app is not enough — see **Safety and When to See a Professional** below.

## Pillar 6 — CONNECT & CHALLENGE: Build Cognitive Reserve 🟢 / 🟡

![Infographic on Pillar 6, connect and challenge: cognitive reserve is a spare fuel tank filled by four pipes - learn a hard new skill, connect with people and teach, keep a purpose, check hearing and eyesight; a schematic graph where the low-reserve curve crosses the decline-shows line earlier than the high-reserve curve; key numbers from the WHO Commission on Social Connection 2025: 1 in 6 people lonely, about 871,000 deaths a year; evidence table: Park 2014 photography or quilting about 16.5 h a week for 3 months improved episodic memory while a social-only group did not, Holt-Lunstad 2010 50% higher odds of survival, Boyle 2010 about 2.4 times as likely to stay free of Alzheimer's; observational links cannot prove cause; social isolation is 5% of dementia cases (Lancet 2024).](diagrams/brain05b_info_connect_reserve_ai.png)

> **Simple version:** The people you talk to, the new things you learn, and having a reason to get up in the morning aren't "soft" extras — they build the brain's **spare capacity** that keeps you sharp as you age. Isolation and boredom, by contrast, are linked to faster decline.

> **Official:** Rich social, intellectual, and physical engagement builds **cognitive reserve**, letting the brain tolerate more age-related change before decline shows (Stern, 2012, *Lancet Neurology*). Social ties matter for health overall: across 148 studies (308,849 people), people with stronger social relationships had **50% higher odds of survival** (Holt-Lunstad et al., 2010, *PLoS Medicine*), and the 2024 Lancet Commission counts **social isolation** among the 14 modifiable risk factors for dementia (see **What to CUT** below). A strong **sense of purpose** is linked to lower Alzheimer's risk: among 951 older adults followed for up to 7 years, people at the 90th percentile of purpose were about **2.4 times as likely to stay free of Alzheimer's** as people at the 10th (Boyle et al., 2010, *Archives of General Psychiatry*). These are observational links, so they cannot prove cause. The best trial evidence is for **learning demanding new skills**: older adults who learned digital photography or quilting (about 16.5 hours a week for 3 months) improved episodic memory, while a social-only group did not (Park et al., 2014, *Psychological Science*).

> **Key number:** loneliness affects about **1 in 6 people** worldwide and is linked to an estimated **871,000 deaths a year** — about 100 every hour (WHO Commission on Social Connection, June 2025).

### What to do

```
   RESERVE-BUILDERS (weave these into normal life)

   • LEARN NEW    The best-tested lever: a hard, unfamiliar skill each season
                  — a language, instrument, sport, or craft. Novelty + difficulty.
   • CONNECT      Regular real conversations, shared activities, teaching others.
                  Teaching forces you to understand deeply (the Feynman effect).
   • PURPOSE      Keep a goal bigger than today's task (why you're upgrading your
                  brain at all). Purpose is linked to a slower-aging mind.
   • HEAR & SEE   Get hearing and eyesight checked and treat any loss — you can't
                  join a conversation you can't hear (see What to CUT).
```

> **Plain English:** stack them. A weekly class with other people — a language, dance, or music group — gives you novelty, difficulty, and connection in one slot.

## Pillar 7 — SUPPLEMENTS: The Honest, Evidence-Tiered List 🟡 / 🔴

![Infographic on Pillar 7, supplements, with a check-with-your-doctor-or-pharmacist caution and a note that US supplements are not FDA-approved before sale; a pyramid on a FOOD FIRST base: Tier 1 fix a real gap (B12, vitamin D, omega-3, strong), Tier 2 small but real (caffeine, creatine, multivitamin 60+, mixed), Tier 3 thin (bacopa, magnesium L-threonate, lion's mane, rhodiola, mixed or weak), Tier 4 skip (ginkgo, nootropic stacks, smart drugs, weak); upper limits vitamin D 4,000 IU a day, magnesium 350 mg a day from supplements, caffeine up to 400 mg a day; trial table: VITAL vitamin D and fish oil no cognitive benefit, creatine memory +0.3 SD, COSMOS multivitamin +0.07 SD, GEM ginkgo 3,069 adults 6.1 years no drop in dementia; interaction checks for blood thinners, metformin and omeprazole, antibiotics and bisphosphonates, losartan; example: age 55, vegan and on metformin means 3 B12 flags, so ask for a B12 test.](diagrams/brain05b_info_supplement_tiers_ai.png)

> **Simple version:** This is deliberately the **last and smallest** pillar. No pill comes close to sleep, exercise, or diet. In the US, dietary supplements are **not approved by the FDA before they are sold** (NIH NCCIH), so most "brain boosters" are marketing. A *few* have real evidence — mostly by **fixing a deficiency**, or by giving a small edge. Rule one: **food first.** Rule two: **don't guess** — supplement a gap you actually have or are at real risk of, and stay under the official upper limits.

> ⚠️ **Talk to a doctor or pharmacist before starting any supplement**, especially if you're pregnant, breastfeeding, on medication, or have a health condition. "Natural" does not mean "safe" or "no interactions." Choose products with a third-party quality seal (e.g., USP, NSF), because contents vary: one lab analysis of 10 "cognitive enhancement" supplements found 5 unapproved drugs, and 9 of the 12 amounts declared on labels were wrong (Cohen et al., 2021, *Neurology: Clinical Practice*).

> **Rule of thumb:** if you are not deficient, the supplements with the best trial results move memory scores by about **0.3 standard deviations** (creatine) or less (a multivitamin in people over 60: 0.07) — useful at the margin, but a garnish, not the meal.

### Tier 1 — Fix a real gap 🟢

The 🟢 is for correcting a deficiency (or, for omega-3, a diet with no fish). If your levels are already fine, none of these works as a brain "booster".

| Supplement | What the evidence shows | Official guidance (adults) |
|------------|-------------------------|----------------------------|
| **Vitamin B12** | Deficiency causes fatigue, numbness, confusion and poor memory, and affects 3–43% of older adults (NIH ODS). Taking B12 when you are *not* deficient did not improve cognition (16 trials, 6,276 people; Markun et al., 2021) | RDA 2.4 mcg/day. Over 50, vegan, or on metformin or acid reducers (e.g., omeprazole)? Ask for a B12 test; fortified foods or supplements absorb well |
| **Vitamin D** | About 1 in 4 people in the US have low or inadequate levels, which harms bones (ODS). But 2,000 IU/day for 2–3 years did not slow cognitive decline in about 4,200 older adults (VITAL; Kang et al., 2021) | RDA 600 IU/day (800 IU after age 70); upper limit 4,000 IU/day unless a clinician is treating a deficiency. Healthy adults under 75 don't need routine testing (Endocrine Society, 2024) |
| **Omega-3 (EPA + DHA)** | Eating fish is linked to healthier brain aging, but fish-oil pills (1 g/day for 2–3 years) did not help cognition in healthy older adults (VITAL; Kang et al., 2022; Cochrane review, 2012) | Eat fatty fish twice a week (Pillar 2). No RDA for EPA/DHA; FDA: at most 5 g/day from supplements. Trials above 1 g/day raised atrial-fibrillation risk (Gencer et al., 2021); bleeding risk with warfarin |

### Tier 2 — Small but real effects 🟡

| Supplement | What the evidence shows | Studied or official amount |
|------------|-------------------------|----------------------------|
| **Caffeine (± L-theanine)** | Caffeine reliably boosts alertness and attention for a few hours. Adding L-theanine (found in tea) gave moderate gains in alertness and attention-switching in the first 2 hours, driven mostly by the caffeine dose (11 trials; Camfield et al., 2014) | FDA: up to 400 mg/day of caffeine is not generally linked to harm in healthy adults (about 2–3 twelve-ounce cups of coffee); if pregnant, the NHS advises no more than 200 mg/day; on medication — ask your clinician. Last cup at least ~9 hours before bed (Pillar 3) |
| **Creatine monohydrate** | Memory improved by about 0.3 SD in two meta-analyses (Prokopidis et al., 2023, 8 trials; Xu et al., 2024, 16 trials and 492 people), with no effect on overall cognition or executive function. Who benefits most is unclear — older adults and vegetarians look most likely (Avgerinos et al., 2018). A published letter questioned the 2023 analysis' statistics | 3–5 g/day in most studies; can add 1–2 kg of water weight. Ask a doctor first if you have kidney disease |
| **Multivitamin (age 60+)** | In the COSMOS trial program (3 sub-studies, about 5,200 adults aged 60+), a daily multivitamin slightly improved global cognition (+0.07 SD) and episodic memory over 2–3 years — roughly 2 years less cognitive aging (Vyas et al., 2024). One trial program; it needs replication | A standard once-daily multivitamin-mineral at about RDA levels |

### Tier 3 — Promising but thin; only if curious 🟡 / 🔴

- 🟡 **Bacopa monnieri** — in 9 trials (518 people, at least 12 weeks) it sped up attention on timed tests; memory results are mixed (Kongkeaw et al., 2014). It can upset the stomach.
- 🟡 **Magnesium** — fix low intake with food first (nuts, seeds, beans, greens, whole grains). The "brain" form, magnesium L-threonate, rests on a few small trials of one branded product (e.g., 100 adults for 6 weeks, 2026) — not yet independently replicated. Better-absorbed general forms: citrate, aspartate, lactate, chloride (ODS). Upper limit from supplements: 350 mg/day.
- 🔴 **Lion's mane** — tiny trials only (30 older adults with mild cognitive impairment, 2009; 41 young adults, 2023, mostly null).
- 🔴 **Rhodiola rosea** — NIH's NCCIH: not enough reliable evidence for any use; may interact with losartan (a blood-pressure drug).

### Tier 4 — Overhyped; save your money 🔴

- **Ginkgo biloba** — the GEM trial gave 3,069 adults aged 75+ ginkgo extract or placebo for a median of 6.1 years: no drop in dementia (hazard ratio 1.12) or Alzheimer's (DeKosky et al., 2008, *JAMA*). It may raise bleeding risk with blood thinners (NCCIH).
- **"Nootropic stacks" and memory pills** — little to no solid evidence in healthy people, and quality is a gamble (see the lab analysis above).
- **Prescription "smart drugs" without a prescription** (modafinil, methylphenidate, amphetamines) — in healthy, rested adults the measured gains are small (overall SMD 0.12 for modafinil, 0.21 for methylphenidate, none for d-amphetamine; Roberts et al., 2020), and side effects are real. Not a study tool.
- **Anything promising a fast "IQ boost"** — not real.

*Amounts are official guidance or what studies used — **not** medical advice; confirm with a clinician.

### Check interactions first

| If you take… | Be careful with… | Why (source) |
|--------------|------------------|--------------|
| Warfarin or other blood thinners | Ginkgo; high-dose omega-3 | Higher bleeding risk (NCCIH; ODS) |
| Metformin, or acid reducers such as omeprazole | (Check your B12) | They lower B12 absorption (ODS) |
| Antibiotics or bisphosphonates (osteoporosis drugs) | Magnesium | Space them apart — magnesium blocks their absorption (ODS) |
| Statins, thiazide diuretics, or steroids | High-dose vitamin D | Statins may work less well; thiazides can push calcium too high; steroids lower vitamin D (ODS) |
| Losartan (blood pressure) | Rhodiola | Reported interaction (NCCIH) |

> **The one-sentence supplement summary:** eat fish and real food, fix any real deficiency (B12 and vitamin D are the usual suspects), use coffee or tea for focus while staying under 400 mg of caffeine a day, try creatine if you're curious — then stop worrying about pills and go for a walk.

## What to CUT (the anti-list)

Sharpening a brain is as much about **removing harm** as adding help. The best map is the **2024 Lancet Commission** on dementia (Livingston et al., 2024, *The Lancet* 404:572–628): 14 risk factors you can change. The large trials that tested changing them are summarised in "How Big Are the Effects? What the Large Trials Show" near the start of this chapter.

> **Key number:** the 14 modifiable risk factors together account for about **45%** of dementia cases worldwide. This is a *population* estimate — the share of cases that might be prevented or delayed if every factor were removed — not a personal guarantee.

|------------|-------------|:--------------:|-----------|
| Early life | Less education | 5% | Keep learning all your life (Pillars 4 and 6) |
| Midlife | Hearing loss | 7% | Hearing test; use hearing aids if needed; limit loud noise |
| Midlife | High LDL cholesterol (new in 2024) | 7% | Get it measured from midlife; treat if high |
| Midlife | Depression | 3% | Get it treated — see Safety below |
| Midlife | Head injury | 3% | Helmets for cycling and contact sports |
| Midlife | Physical inactivity | 2% | Pillar 1 |
| Midlife | Diabetes | 2% | Check blood sugar; treat it |
| Midlife | Smoking | 2% | Stop; ask for cessation support |
| Midlife | High blood pressure | 2% | Keep systolic pressure at 130 mm Hg or lower from age 40 |
| Midlife | Obesity | 1% | Pillars 1 and 2 |
| Midlife | Excessive alcohol | 1% | Drink less — see below |
| Late life | Social isolation | 5% | Pillar 6 |
| Late life | Air pollution | 3% | Mostly a policy issue; avoid smoky air where you can |

**Hearing aids, honestly:** in the ACHIEVE trial (977 adults aged 70–84 with hearing loss), hearing aids did **not** slow 3-year cognitive decline overall, but a pre-specified analysis suggested a benefit in the higher-risk group (Lin et al., 2023, *The Lancet*). Treat hearing loss anyway — it is the gateway to Pillar 6.

**Alcohol:** the Lancet risk factor is *excessive* drinking, but the World Health Organization's 2023 statement is blunter: "no level of alcohol consumption is safe for our health" — the risk starts from the first drop and rises with every drink. For the brain, **less is better, and none is best**.

```
   EVERYDAY BRAIN-DRAINERS (covered in Pillars 1–5)

   ✗ Chronic short sleep        ✗ Ultra-processed / high-sugar diet
   ✗ Sitting all day            ✗ Chronic unmanaged stress
   ✗ Dehydration                ✗ Fragmented attention (constant scrolling)
   ✗ Untreated sleep disorders such as sleep apnea
```

Fixing a real risk factor — blood pressure, hearing, smoking — is a bigger lever than any pill in Pillar 7.

## The 12-Week Starter Program

Don't try to install all seven pillars on day one — that's how people burn out and quit. **Add one focus per phase**, keep the earlier ones going, and by week 12 the whole system is running on autopilot. Structure helps: in the 2-year US POINTER trial, a structured lifestyle program beat a self-guided one on global cognition, though the difference was modest (see "How Big Are the Effects?" near the start of this chapter).

> **Rule of thumb:** one new focus every 4 weeks. By week 12 you hit the WHO minimum — **150 minutes of moderate activity a week + strength on 2 days** — plus 7–9 hours of sleep, a daily Anki habit, and 10 minutes of calm.

```
   PHASE 1  WEEKS 1–4   FOUNDATIONS   Sleep + Move + Water + cut the worst junk
   PHASE 2  WEEKS 5–8   BUILD         Add the brain-healthy plate + daily mental training
   PHASE 3  WEEKS 9–12  OPTIMIZE      Add strength, meditation, a new hard skill,
                                      social/purpose, and (only for a real gap) supplements
        ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
        Throughout: everything from earlier phases keeps running. Layer, don't restart.
```

| Phase | Weeks | Install (new focus) | Keep going | Realistic win by end |
|-------|-------|---------------------|------------|----------------------|
| **1 — Foundations** | 1–4 | Fixed wake time + 7–9 h sleep; brisk walks building to 150 min/week (e.g., 5 × 30 min); water in reach; cut sodas/junk; book a check-up (blood pressure, LDL, blood sugar, hearing, eyesight) | — | Clearer head, steadier energy & mood |
| **2 — Build** | 5–8 | Brain-healthy (Mediterranean/MIND-style) plate; a small daily Anki deck (2–4 of your own new cards a day) + 1 deep read/week | Phase 1 habits | Better retention; food + habit locked in |
| **3 — Optimize** | 9–12 | Strength 2×/week; 10-min daily meditation; a new hard skill; regular social time + a clear "why"; supplements only for a confirmed gap (Pillar 7) | Phases 1–2 | Fitter, calmer, faster learning; full system live |

### A sustainable weekly template (from Phase 3 onward)

```
   MON  Brisk walk 30m · deep read (your 1 per week)
   TUE  Strength · new-skill practice
   WED  Brisk walk 30m · social / teach someone
   THU  Strength · new-skill practice
   FRI  Brisk walk 30m · new-skill practice
   SAT  Long walk/hike in nature 60m · relaxed social
   SUN  Rest from training · reflect · plan the week · prep meals
        EVERY DAY: sleep 7–9 h · brain-healthy food · Anki · 10-min meditation
        WEEKLY TOTAL: 150 min moderate activity + 2 strength days
```

**Check the template against WHO:** 3 × 30-minute walks + a 60-minute Saturday hike = **150 moderate minutes**, plus strength on Tuesday and Thursday = **2 strength days** — exactly the WHO 2020 minimum. Three 25-minute jogs plus the same two strength days would also qualify (75 vigorous minutes ≈ 150 moderate, since one vigorous minute counts as about two), while five 20-minute walks (100 minutes, no strength) would fall short on both counts. Run your own week through the checker in **Pillar 1 → What to actually do**.

> **Beyond 12 weeks — the maintenance dose:** drop the "installing" mindset and just *live* it. The habits that compound are the ones you keep: a daily walk, nightly good sleep, brain-healthy eating, a running Anki deck, and one hard thing you're always learning.

## Myths and Failure Modes (and the Fix)

| Myth / mistake | Why it's wrong | Do this instead |
|----------------|----------------|-----------------|
| "This pill/app will boost my IQ 15 points" | No evidence; fluid intelligence is largely stable in adults | Optimize the brain you have; slow its aging |
| Brain-training games make you smarter | Gains don't transfer to general ability (Owen et al., 2010; Simons et al., 2016) | Learn a real hard skill; drill actual reasoning/recall |
| Cutting sleep to study more | A sleep-deprived brain forms new memories worse (Yoo et al., 2007) | Protect sleep FIRST, then study |
| "I'll fix it all with supplements" | Smallest lever; vitamin D and fish-oil pills didn't help cognition in VITAL | Move, eat, sleep first; pills are a garnish |
| "More is better" with vitamins | Above the upper limit is harm, not extra benefit | Stay under 4,000 IU/day vitamin D and 350 mg/day supplemental magnesium unless a clinician says otherwise |
| Highlighting & re-reading | Rated "low utility" study methods (Dunlosky et al., 2013) | Close the book, recall from memory |
| "Detox teas / miracle nootropics" | No evidence; some "nootropics" contain unapproved drugs | Real food + exercise; bloodwork only if you're at risk |
| Trying all 7 pillars perfectly at once | Overwhelm → quit | One phase at a time; layer over 12 weeks |
| "I'm too old to change my brain" | Exercise grew the hippocampus in adults aged 55–80 (Erickson et al., 2011) | Start now — gains happen at every age |

## Safety and When to See a Professional

This program is for generally healthy people who want to sharpen and protect their minds. It is **not** a medical treatment.

> ⚠️ **See a doctor if you notice:** persistent or worsening memory loss, confusion, or trouble with everyday tasks; low mood, hopelessness, or loss of interest lasting 2+ weeks; chronic exhaustion despite adequate sleep, or heavy snoring/gasping (possible sleep apnea); new trouble hearing or seeing. These need professional care, not a productivity plan. Also consult a clinician **before** starting supplements or a big new exercise regimen if you have any health condition, are pregnant/breastfeeding, or take medication.

> **If you are struggling right now:** if you ever have thoughts of harming yourself, please reach out today — call your local emergency number or a crisis line. Examples: in the US, call or text **988** (988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, 24/7, free); in India, call **Tele-MANAS 14416** (or 1-800-891-4416). Talking to someone helps, and you don't have to handle it alone.

The deeper cognitive science is in **[Chapter 5 — Brain Training](#content/05_brain_training)**; this chapter is the **whole-body program**. Move, eat, and sleep well; train your mind on real skills; stay calm and connected; fix the risk factors you can; add a supplement only for a real gap. Do it for months and your brain works closer to its ceiling — and you give it its best odds of staying sharp for years.

**Evidence to remember (as of November 2026):**

- **Lifestyle trials:** effects are real but small — FINGER (2015) +0.022 SD a year vs health advice; US POINTER (JAMA 2025, 2,111 adults, 2 years) structured 0.243 vs self-guided 0.213 SD a year in global cognition.
- **Exercise:** small-to-moderate cognitive gains across 2,724 trials (general cognition SMD 0.42; Singh et al., 2025); it mainly protects the hippocampus from shrinking rather than reliably growing it (Firth et al., 2018).
- **Diet:** the MIND diet's strong links are observational; its 3-year randomized trial found no significant difference vs a control diet (Barnes et al., NEJM 2023).
- **Risk factors:** 14 modifiable factors ≈ 45% of dementia cases (Lancet Commission 2024 — a population estimate); hearing loss and high LDL lead at 7% each; WHO 2023: no safe level of alcohol.
- **Stress and people:** mindfulness cuts distress by about 0.3 SD; loneliness affects 1 in 6 people (WHO, 2025); learning a demanding skill improved memory where socialising alone did not.
- **Supplements:** vitamin D (2,000 IU) and fish-oil (1 g) pills didn't help cognition in VITAL; creatine moves memory by about 0.3 SD; ginkgo failed in GEM — fix real gaps, respect upper limits, check interactions.

**Quick self-check** (answers in italics):

1. In US POINTER, what did the structured program add over the self-guided one — and why can't we say how much either helped versus doing nothing? *About +0.029 SD a year in global cognition (0.243 vs 0.213); there was no do-nothing group, so part of both gains is practice on the tests.*
2. Does 3 × 25 minutes of jogging plus 2 strength sessions meet the WHO 2020 guidance? *Yes — 75 vigorous minutes × 2 = 150 moderate-equivalent minutes, plus strength on 2 days.*
3. Which two risk factors did the Lancet Commission add in 2024, and how big are they? *High LDL cholesterol (7%) and untreated vision loss (2%).*
4. Why is a vitamin D pill not a brain booster for most people? *In VITAL, 2,000 IU a day for 2–3 years did not slow cognitive decline; get the RDA (600–800 IU), stay under 4,000 IU a day, and treat only a real deficiency.*
